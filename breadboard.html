Breadboard basics

Most failed lab circuits are not logic mistakes. They are a chip on the wrong side of the channel, a rail that is not powered, or a wire one hole off. This page is about those.

Inside the board

Under the plastic are metal spring clips. Every hole in a clip is the same electrical point, so a wire anywhere in that clip connects to everything else in it.

  • Terminal strips run vertically: holes a–e in one column are joined, and f–j in the same column are joined, but the two halves are separate.
  • The centre channel splits those halves. That gap is exactly the width of a DIP chip, so each pin gets its own strip.
  • Power rails run the whole length along the top and bottom, one red (+) and one blue (−).
  • On many long boards the rails are split in the middle. If the right half of your circuit is dead, bridge the gap with a short wire.

Placing a chip

A DIP chip always straddles the centre channel. Put it flat on one half and pins on the same column short together.

  • Find the notch (or the dot). With the notch on your left, pin 1 is bottom-left.
  • Pins count anti-clockwise: along the bottom left to right, then back along the top right to left.
  • So on a 14-pin chip, pin 7 is bottom-right and pin 14 is top-left, right above pin 1.
  • Press it in evenly. A bent leg folded under the chip looks fine from above and does nothing.

Power pins

Most chips follow the corner rule: the top-left pin is VCC and the bottom-right pin is GND. A few famous ones do not, and they are favourite viva questions.

Swap VCC and GND and a real TTL chip gets hot within seconds. The logic bench marks it as burnt and makes you place a new one, so you remember.

TTL logic levels

A 74LS input does not need exactly 0 V or 5 V. Anything below 0.8 V counts as LOW and anything above 2 V counts as HIGH. In between is undefined.

  • Outputs promise better than that: LOW is at most 0.4 V and HIGH is at least 2.7 V. The gap is the noise margin (0.4 V for 74LS).
  • A floating TTL input usually reads as HIGH, but it picks up noise. Never rely on it; tie unused inputs to a rail.
  • One 74LS output can drive about 20 74LS inputs (its fan-out).

Wire colours

Wire colour does nothing electrically, but a tidy board is one you can debug. The logic bench uses this scheme when wire colour is set to automatic.

  • Red +5 V and anything tied HIGH
  • Black ground and anything tied LOW
  • Yellow inputs from switches and pulsers
  • Green outputs to LEDs and the display
  • Purple clock lines
  • Blue, orange, white connections between chips

LEDs and active-low outputs

An LED needs a resistor in series or it burns out. Pick R = (Vs − Vf) / I. With 5 V, a red LED (Vf ≈ 1.8 V) and 10 mA, that is 320 Ω, so use 330 Ω. Trainer LEDs already have their resistor built in, which is why you can wire straight to them.

Some chips mark outputs with a bar, like Y0 on the 74138. That means active-low: the selected output goes to 0, so its LED turns off while the others stay lit. It is not a fault.

When it doesn't work

Go in this order. It moves from the cheapest check to the most expensive, and most faults are found in the first three steps.

  1. Is the trainer on, and are the rails live? Touch the logic probe to each rail. Red rail HIGH, blue rail LOW.
  2. Is every chip powered? Probe the VCC pin and the GND pin of each chip, not the rail next to it.
  3. Is every chip across the channel and the right way round? Notch on the left, every leg in its own strip.
  4. Are the inputs arriving? Flip each switch and probe the chip pin it should reach. If the probe says FLOAT, the wire is in the wrong strip.
  5. Follow the signal one gate at a time. Probe each gate output and compare with the truth table. The first wrong output is where the fault is.
  6. Check for two outputs on one node. Two outputs wired together fight; the probe shows an in-between level (CLASH on the logic bench).

Pinout library

Each chip is drawn the way it sits on the breadboard: notch on the left, pin 1 bottom-left. The table under each drawing lists which pins are inputs and which are outputs, gate by gate. A bar over a name means active-low.
